The Diversity Prediction Theorem

crowd error² = average individual error² − diversity²

The crowd is always at least as good as its average member, and the more the guesses disagree, the bigger the edge. Herding shrinks the diversity, so the edge disappears.

The ox at Plymouth

In 1906 the statistician Francis Galton visited the West of England Fat Stock and Poultry Exhibition. A weight-judging contest was running: for sixpence you could write your name and your guess for the weight of a live ox, after it had been slaughtered and dressed.

Galton, a believer in the superiority of the expert and an enthusiast for measuring everything, collected the tickets afterward, expecting to demonstrate how poor the average person's judgment was. About 800 people entered, and 787 tickets were usable.

The ox weighed 1,198 lbs. The crowd's median guess was 1,207 lbs and the mean was 1,197 lbs, one pound off. Most individuals did worse. Galton reported it in Nature in 1907 under the title Vox Populi ("the voice of the people") and admitted the result was "more creditable to the trustworthiness of a democratic judgment than might have been expected."

Why it works

Each person's guess is the truth plus an error. If those errors are independent and don't all lean the same way, they cancel when you average. Some people guess high, some low, and the average keeps the part they all share: the signal.

James Surowiecki's The Wisdom of Crowds lists four conditions:

  1. Diversity of opinion: people bring different information and intuitions.
  2. Independence: guesses aren't swayed by those of others.
  3. Decentralization: people draw on local, specialized knowledge.
  4. Aggregation: something turns many guesses into one answer.

Remove one and the magic fails. Try it in the Crowd Lab: add peer influence and the guesses clump together while the average drifts away from the truth, or add a shared bias and a million guessers all converge on the wrong answer.

About this app

The crowds in this app are simulated: each of the 800 guessers is drawn from a log-normal distribution around the real answer, with a different amount of noise and shared bias for each question. The original 1906 tickets don't survive in this form, but the statistics above are Galton's.
